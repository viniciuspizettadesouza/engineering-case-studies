# Interview Strategy and Team Evaluation

An interview is a two-way evaluation. Prepare evidence about your work, but also collect enough information to decide whether the role, team, and business are healthy.

## Three questions to always ask

1. What are the team's or project's biggest challenges today?
2. How will success and performance be evaluated in this role?
3. What is the plan for the team or product over the next six to twelve months?

## Understand the role

- What would I own, and where does that responsibility fit in the team's plan?
- What is the product's current level of technical maturity?
- What should a new team member understand before starting?
- What does the development flow look like from planning through delivery?
- How are priorities, estimates, and deadlines decided?

Ask for concrete examples. A useful answer describes recent work, decision owners, constraints, and what the team learned—not only an idealized process.

## Success, growth, and retention

- Which outcomes or signals are used in performance reviews?
- What distinguishes a solid first three months from an excellent first year?
- How do promotion and career-development cycles work?
- How does the organization help engineers grow between levels?
- How stable is the team, and why have people recently joined or left?

## Culture and autonomy

- How are tasks distributed day to day?
- What level of initiative and ownership is expected?
- How does onboarding work, especially for remote contributors?
- How does the team handle disagreement?
- Which recurring meetings exist, and which decisions happen asynchronously?
- How is communication handled across teams and time zones?

## Technical environment

- Are responsibilities clear across engineering, product, design, and quality?
- How does the team test code, review changes, and operate CI/CD?
- Which parts of the system create the most delivery risk?
- How does the team decide when to refactor or replace technology?
- How is artificial intelligence used, reviewed, and governed?
- Can developers install the tools required to do their work safely?

Healthy teams can discuss trade-offs. Warning signs include recurring incidents with no follow-up, business rules duplicated across modules, tightly coupled components, unclear naming, very large units with mixed responsibilities, and pressure to ship without tests or review. One imperfect function is not evidence of a broken culture; repeated behavior and the team's response matter more.

## Business and employment terms

When appropriate for the interview stage, ask:

- How does the organization make money, and what is its funding horizon?
- Who influences product priorities?
- What creates defensible value for customers?
- How do salary, bonus, equity, leave, and learning budgets work?
- Is open-source contribution supported?
- What location, travel, or office attendance is required?

## Presenting your experience

Prefer short evidence over adjectives. Use a situation, the constraint, your action, and the result. Explain what you personally decided while crediting collaborators.

For remote adaptability, prepare examples of learning an unfamiliar stack, joining without perfect onboarding, changing collaboration tools, working across time zones, documenting decisions, and raising blockers early. A concise positioning statement is:

> I work calmly and pragmatically, take responsibility for outcomes, communicate risks early, and adapt quickly when the context changes.

Avoid promising unlimited ownership or implying that colleagues never need to be involved. Senior ownership includes making work visible, asking for context, and bringing the right people into decisions.

## Practice checklist

Be ready to explain a difficult task, the work you enjoy, and examples involving asynchronous JavaScript, component communication, input validation, TypeScript, accessibility, architecture, and collaboration. Use the technical chapters and exercises in this handbook to prepare detailed answers.
